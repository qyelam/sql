This article was an interesting read and brought up themes that I encounter on a daily basis as a social psychologist, especially in a world trending towards categorization and separation. In particular, it was interesting to read about Pakistan’s registry, since I don’t have much background with large databases or how information is encoded and retrieved from said databases. However, I am very familiar with the idea that categories are mostly social constructs, such as gender, race, family, socioeconomic status, etc. 

When I design my studies, I have to think critically about the choices I make when collecting data, about how to collect and quantify my data, since the operationalizations of my constructs will necessarily constrain how the data can be analyzed. For example, if I am interested in socioeconomic status, it could be measure objectively by indicators such as raw income, range of income, education or subjectively by indicators like “middle class” or asking people to place themselves on a ladder from 0 to 10 where 10 is the richest. Each of these indicators will have different implications for the results of my analysis, and additionally, the options I provide for people to answer about each indicator also matters. For example, it is well known in psychology research that objective and subjective measures of socioeconomic status do not correlate very highly. Additionally, I could define “range of income” in buckets of $50,000 vs buckets of $10,000, or create buckets such as “less than $50k” and “more than 100k”. The choices that I make with cut-offs and the granularity of my buckets may have real impacts on decisions as nuance can be lost. For example, if there was a huge difference in quality in life of those making under $30k and those making $30k - $40k, making the bucket “less than $50k” would entirely lose this nuance and we might come to conclusions from our data analysis that don’t reflect reality for those making less than $30k. Every piece of information about a human being that is turned into a number requires effortful thinking and process. It is not a simple 1 to 1 process. In psychology, we call these kinds of ambiguous decisions “researcher degrees of freedom”. They can have very real impacts on the results that we find.

Similarly, as a mixed-methods researcher, it is glaringly apparent to me that the trade-off of quantifying information by turning it into numbers that can be easily analyzed is losing nuance and variability from qualitative data. Real life data is messy and hard to quantify, as suggested in the article by alternative family structures or gender identities. For example, when coding qualitative data, researchers have to make the choice of what themes or categories to create and at what level of detail, At some point, if a thought is not brought up enough times, it may not make the cut for its own category. So, some outliers may be lost. This is likely part of what happened in the Pakistan database and happens very frequently with marginalized communities, who experience all kinds of invisibility on all levels, including structural or systemic. An easy example in North America is the trouble transgender individuals can experience when it comes to changing their gender on legal documents, and having those legal documents be perpetually questioned.

Though not exactly a database or data system, medical data is also rife with this, as the baseline comparison group for pretty much all treatment and conditions is white men. Diagnostic criteria for most conditions is based on how the condition presents in white men, even though it may present differently for other groups of people. Relatedly, insurance is probably a database and database system that is rife with all sorts of values imbedded in it. Though largely driven by profit margins, the norms of a “healthy individual” are based on all sorts of prototypes like young white man, or what a prototypical healthy individual at the age of 40 is.

I could probably speak at quite a length about how all sorts of socially constructed norms permeate society, but for the sake of brevity, I will wrap up with the reflection that the more you look, the more you can see, every norm or value or system is not objectively created, but based on some subjective set of values, often chosen by whoever is in power at the time to legitimize and normalize conditions favouring themselves as the moral high ground.
